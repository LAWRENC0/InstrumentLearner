# Guitar

Everything specific to the six-string guitar in standard tuning: the fretboard, shapes, scale patterns and the technique of both hands. Read the music theory file alongside this one. In this file, "string 1" is the high E, as in the app.

How to use this file: learn the fretboard maps first, then the shapes, then the technique. The **Styles and progressions** group applies the theory file's progression library to the guitar: blues, jazz, Brazilian, flamenco and rhythm guitar across styles. Every technique has an exercise. Play them slowly with a metronome; speed comes last.

## Standard tuning and string numbers
@group Fretboard

{{notes: E A D G B E | guitar piano}}


- Strings from low to high: E2, A2, D3, G3, B3, E4. The guitar sounds an octave lower than it is written on the staff (the usual notation is written an octave higher).
- The app numbers strings 1 (high E) to 6 (low E). In real life guitarists often count the other way. Be careful when a teacher or tab uses a different convention.
- A **capo** raises all open strings. A capo on fret 2 makes the open strings sound like D A D G B E tuned up a whole step.
- Tuning drop D (low E down to D) gives the low string a root that sits below the open chord shapes.

Exercise: play each open string and sing its note name. Then play the open strings again, this time naming the interval between each pair (perfect 4ths, except G to B, a major 3rd).

## Fret = semitone
@group Fretboard

{{notes: E F F# G | guitar piano}}


- Each fret is one semitone higher than the one before. Fret 12 is an octave above the open string, fret 5 is a perfect 4th, fret 7 is a perfect 5th.
- The fret markers are single dots at 3, 5, 7, 9 and 15, and a double dot at 12.
- Frets get closer as you go up the neck. Each fret is about 5.6 % narrower than the one before, because of equal temperament.
- Pressing a string just behind the fret wire gives a clean note with the least effort. Pressing in the middle of the fret goes flat, and pressing too far back can mute.

Exercise: play fret 12 on each string, then fret 7, and sing the note you hear. Confirm the octave and the fifth against the open strings.

## Note maps for every string (frets 0 to 12)

- **String 6 (E2)**: E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E
- **String 5 (A2)**: A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A
- **String 4 (D3)**: D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B C C♯ D
- **String 3 (G3)**: G G♯ A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G
- **String 2 (B3)**: B C C♯ D D♯ E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B
- **String 1 (E4)**: E F F♯ G G♯ A A♯ B C C♯ D D♯ E

Memory tip: the natural notes on string 6 (E F G A B C D E) appear at frets 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12. Those are your landmarks.

Exercise: write the note map for all six strings from memory, then check each row against this list. Do it again from fret 12 down.

## Intervals between strings
@group Fretboard

{{notes: C E | guitar piano}}
{{notes: C G | guitar piano}}


- Moving from one string up to the next (toward string 1), the open note rises by 5 semitones, except from string 3 (G) to string 2 (B), which rises by 4.
- To find an interval on an adjacent string: new fret = old fret + (interval in semitones − string step). The string step is 5, or 4 for the G–B pair.
- Example: from string 5 fret 3 (C) to string 4, the major 3rd (4 semitones) lands on fret 3 + 4 − 5 = 2, which is E. C to E is a major 3rd ✓.
- On one string: 2 frets is a whole tone, 3 frets a minor 3rd, 4 frets a major 3rd, 5 frets a perfect 4th, 7 frets a perfect 5th, 12 frets an octave.
- The same formula works across two strings apart: subtract two string steps (10, or 9 across G–B).

Exercise: pick any note on string 6 and find its perfect 5th, major 3rd and minor 7th on strings 5, 4 and 3, using the formula. Check each answer on the note map.

## Octaves and unisons
@group Fretboard

{{notes: C | guitar piano}}


- An octave shape is the same letter name two strings across, one octave higher. From string 6 open (E2), the octave is string 4 fret 2 (E3): two strings toward string 1, and two frets higher. It is useful for playing a melody with two voices.
- Unisons across strings: the same note on two strings, for example string 5 fret 5 (D) and string 4 open (D).
- Octave shapes give a fuller sound and make it easy to double a melody.

Exercise: play the octave shape of each note of the C major scale on strings 6 and 4. Then do the same on 5 and 3.

## Triads: string sets and inversions
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: G C D Em | guitar piano}}
{{chords: G G/B G/D | guitar piano}}


- A triad uses three different notes on three adjacent strings. Typical sets: strings 1–2–3, 2–3–4, 3–4–5, and 4–5–6.
- The three notes of a triad, arranged lowest to highest, make its inversion. The lowest note is the bass.
- Example: G major on strings 3, 2 and 1: open string 3 (G), open string 2 (B), fret 3 on string 1 (D). Root position.
- First inversion of G major on strings 3, 2 and 1: string 3 fret 4 (B, the lowest note, which is the third of G), string 2 fret 3 (D, the fifth), string 1 fret 3 (G, the octave). The bass is the third, so this is first inversion.
- To build any triad voicing: list the chord tones, choose the inversion (which note is lowest), then find each note on the string set, in order from low to high. Check each note on the note map.
- Minor triads lower the 3rd by one fret; diminished lowers the 3rd and 5th; augmented raises the 5th.
- Triads sounding on adjacent strings sound thinner than drop-2 voicings across four strings. Use them when the rhythm section already covers the bass.

Exercise: for each string set (1–2–3, 2–3–4, 3–4–5, 4–5–6) play a major triad on C, then a minor triad on C. Name each note and its role.

## Seventh chords and drop-2 voicings
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: Cmaj7 G7 Dm7 | guitar piano}}


- A **drop-2 voicing** takes the second-highest note of a close chord and drops it an octave. On guitar this usually means skipping a string, so the chord sits across four strings.
- Example: Cmaj7 close is C E G B (low to high). Drop 2 takes the second-highest note (G) and drops it an octave, giving G C E B from low to high. On strings 4 to 1: string 4 fret 5 (G), string 3 fret 5 (C), string 2 fret 5 (E), string 1 fret 7 (B). Three notes sit on the same fret, which makes the shape easy to see.
- Common 7th shapes: the E-shape maj7 and m7, the A-shape dominant 7, the D-shape m7. These are movable.
- **Shell voicings** keep only the 3rd and 7th: for a G7 the shell is B and F. Three notes on three strings, very clear in a band.

Exercise: play a ii–V–I (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7) using drop-2 voicings on strings 5–2 for each chord. Keep the common notes in the same string.

## Extended chords: 9, 11, 13
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: G9 G11 G13 | guitar piano}}


- A **9th chord** adds the 9th (2nd) above the 7th. On guitar the 9th is often played on the top string, one or two frets above the root.
- Dominant 9 shape: strings 6–1 with the root low, the 3rd and 7th in the middle, the 9th on top.
- **11th and 13th** chords are dense. Omit the 3rd or the 11th to keep clarity: a 13th chord usually leaves out the 11th.
- Practice: play the 9 chord, then the 9 without the 5th, then the 13 without the 11th. Hear the difference.

Exercise: write the dominant 9, 11 and 13 chords on the string set 4–1 for the root G. List which chord tones you chose to leave out and why.

## Power chords
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: A5 E5 | guitar piano}}


- A **power chord** is a root and a fifth, played on two or three strings. It has no 3rd, so it is neither major nor minor.
- Shapes: the E power chord (string 6 fret n, string 5 fret n + 2, string 4 fret n + 2), the A power chord (string 5 fret n, string 4 fret n + 2, string 3 fret n + 2).
- Play them with the first and third finger, or the first and the fourth in a stretch. Use down-strokes for rock and metal.
- Distortion amplifies the 5th and the octave, which is why power chords sound strong. Clean power chords sound thin.

Exercise: play the power chord on each string from the root A (string 6 fret 5, string 5 fret 7, string 4 fret 7). Then move it up and down the neck, chromatically.

## Barre chords
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: F Bb Gm | guitar piano}}


- A **barre chord** uses one finger as a movable nut across several strings. Choose a barre shape (E or A) and move it: the root follows the barred fret.
- **E-shape barre** (from the open E major, 0 2 2 1 0 0): with the barre at fret n, string 6 is fret n (root), string 5 is fret n+2 (fifth), string 4 is fret n+2 (octave), string 3 is fret n+1 (third), strings 2 and 1 are fret n. Name the chord by the root on string 6 (fret 5 = A, fret 7 = B).
- **A-shape barre** (from the open A major, x 0 2 2 2 0): with the barre at fret n, string 5 is fret n (root), string 4 is fret n+2 (fifth), string 3 is fret n+2 (octave), string 2 is fret n+2 (third), string 1 is fret n. Name the chord by the root on string 5.
- Technique: place the barre just behind the fret, with the bony edge of the index finger, not the pad. Keep the thumb behind the neck, around the middle of its back, for leverage.
- Tension: if the barre buzzes, roll the finger slightly toward the nut or raise the elbow.

Exercise: play the E-shape major barre at frets 1 to 12 (F to E) and the A-shape at frets 1 to 12. Name each chord as you play it.

## The CAGED system
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: C G A E D | guitar piano}}


- The **CAGED** system links five open chord shapes (C, A, G, E, D) to five positions on the neck. Each shape can be moved up and down with a barre, and the scale or arpeggio that goes with each shape covers the neck in a repeating pattern.
- Use CAGED to find a chord or scale in any key: find the open shape, slide it to the root you want, and the pattern follows.
- Example: G major is available as the open G shape, the E shape at fret 3, the D shape at fret 5, the C shape at fret 7, and the A shape at fret 10. Each is the same chord, with a different voicing.

Exercise: play a C major chord in each of the five CAGED shapes, all over the neck, then name the position.

## Scale positions (three notes per string)
@group Scales and positions

{{scale: C major | guitar piano}}


- Major scales are often played with three notes on each string in a movable pattern. This is the most common fingering for lead playing, because it is tidy and logical.
- Example: C major in a three-notes-per-string position, starting on the low E at fret 8 (C): string 6 frets 8, 10, 12 (C D E); string 5 frets 8, 10, 12 (F G A); string 4 frets 9, 10, 12 (B C D); string 3 frets 9, 10, 12 (E F G); string 2 frets 1, 3, 5 (C D E); string 1 frets 1, 3, 5 (F G A). Check the notes against the note maps before you play it.
- The pattern moves to any key by shifting every note the same number of frets (a whole tone up is two frets, and so on).
- Fingering: use one finger per fret. On a string with three notes, play 1, 3, 4 on the first string, then shift to 1, 2, 4 on the next.
- Modes are the same notes as the parent scale, played from a different starting note. Dorian from D is the C major pattern started on the second degree.

Exercise: play the C major scale in one position up and down, naming each note. Then play the D Dorian from the same shape by starting on D.

## Pentatonic scales in depth
@group Scales and positions

{{scale: A minor pentatonic | guitar piano}}
{{scale: A blues | guitar piano}}


- The **minor pentatonic** (1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7) is the core blues and rock scale. Its five boxes cover the whole neck.
- Box 1 for A minor (root A on string 6 fret 5): string 6 frets 5 and 8 (A, C); string 5 frets 5 and 7 (D, E); string 4 frets 5 and 7 (G, A); string 3 frets 5 and 7 (C, D); string 2 frets 5 and 8 (E, G); string 1 frets 5 and 8 (A, C).
- The five boxes connect at their shared notes. Box 1 is the lowest position; each box uses the next root note.
- The **major pentatonic** of a root uses the same notes as the minor pentatonic of the note three semitones below it. So C major pentatonic is played with the A minor boxes, starting from the C.
- The **blues scale** adds the ♭5 to the minor pentatonic. Play it as the minor pentatonic box with the ♭5 added between the 4th and the 5th.

Exercise: play box 1 of A minor pentatonic up and down, then play it in the other four boxes. Then play the blues scale from the same box, adding the ♭5 between the 4th and 5th.

## Scales in other positions and modes
@group Scales and positions

{{scale: D dorian | guitar piano}}
{{scale: G mixolydian | guitar piano}}


- Each mode can be played in any position. Use the root note of the mode to find the box, then count the notes of the mode from the root.
- Dorian example: D Dorian is the C major shape started on D. Frets are the same as the C major pattern, the sound changes because the tonic changes.
- The harmonic minor and melodic minor shapes are one fret different from the major pattern: lower the 3rd, then the 6th or 7th, as the mode requires.
- Use the modes over chords: Dm7 with D Dorian, G7 with G Mixolydian (the major pattern with the ♭7), Cmaj7 with C Lydian (the major pattern with the ♯4).

Exercise: over a Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 progression, play D Dorian, G Mixolydian and C Lydian from the same string pattern, moving with the chords.

## Picking hand: how to hold the pick
@group Picking

- Hold the pick between the thumb and the side of the index finger, with the pick resting on the pad of the thumb. The index finger rests on the pick's edge and bends slightly at the knuckle.
- Keep the grip light: the pick should be held firmly enough not to slip, but not gripped like a vice. Tension in the wrist and forearm is the most common cause of fatigue and poor tone.
- The exposed tip is usually a few millimetres. A longer tip sounds brighter and gives more attack; a shorter tip sounds warmer and is easier to control.
- Pick angle: the pick is nearly parallel to the string, with the tip angled so that its leading edge strikes the string. Too flat and the pick scrapes; too steep and it catches.
- Pick thickness changes everything: thin picks (0.5 to 0.6 mm) bend and are good for strumming; medium (0.7 to 0.8 mm) suit most players; thick (1 mm and above) give control for lead and metal.

Exercise: hold the pick with the grip described above and play open strings with a down-stroke at 60 BPM for one minute. Stop if the forearm tenses; keep the wrist loose and the motion from the wrist and elbow.

## Picking motion and anchoring
@group Picking

- Anchor the picking hand on the bridge or the lower body of the guitar (the pinky or the side of the hand). The anchor gives control; the picking comes from the wrist and forearm, and the fingers stay still.
- The stroke is a small arc: the pick moves in and out of the string, not from the arm alone. Keep the motion short for speed.
- Strumming: the wrist and forearm move together. Keep the hand steady and relaxed; loose wrists give a more natural rhythm.

Exercise: play a steady eighth-note pattern on the open E string (down, up, down, up) for 2 minutes, keeping the wrist still and the forearm moving.

## Alternate picking
@group Picking

{{scale: C major | guitar piano}}


- **Alternate picking** alternates down and up strokes on every note. It is the standard technique for single-note lines.
- Rules: the down-stroke starts on the beat, the up-stroke fills the gap. Keep the picking hand moving even when the notes are not played (for rests, keep the motion going).
- Start slowly. Play each note with the same volume; increase the tempo only when the sound is even.

Exercise: play the C major scale in eighth notes at 80 BPM with strict alternate picking. Then do the same at 100, 120 and 140 BPM, one step at a time.

## Economy picking
@group Picking

- **Economy picking** uses the direction of the pick to choose the stroke, so that you move from one string to the next with the least motion. When the next note is on a lower string, use a down-stroke; when it is on a higher string, use an up-stroke, if the notes are in a single direction.
- It is faster than alternate picking on scale patterns that cross strings. It needs practice to keep the sound even.
- Example: a three-note pattern on strings 3 to 1 (notes going up across strings): use a down-stroke, then up, and the rest follows the direction of the notes.

Exercise: play the C major box (three notes per string) with economy picking, then with alternate picking. Compare the speed and the evenness.

## Sweep picking
@group Picking

{{chords: Am7 | guitar piano}}


- **Sweep picking** plays an arpeggio across several strings with one continuous motion of the pick, down or up, without changing the direction of the stroke.
- Technique: the pick glides across the strings. Lift the fretting fingers to create a single pick stroke on each string, and keep the stroke light.
- Practice: play a minor arpeggio across three strings with one down-stroke, then the reverse with one up-stroke.

Exercise: sweep an Am7 arpeggio (A C E G) across strings 4–2, slowly, one stroke per note.

## Hybrid picking
@group Picking

{{chords: G | guitar piano}}


- **Hybrid picking** uses the pick for the main notes and the middle or ring fingers of the picking hand for notes on other strings. Common in country (the "chicken picking" style) and in jazz guitar.
- Grip: the pick is held as usual, with the middle finger and ring finger free to pluck the higher strings.
- Practice: play a country lick with the pick on the low strings and the middle finger on the high strings.

Exercise: play the root and fifth of a G major chord with the pick on the low strings and the middle finger on the high G and B strings. Practise the switch until it is smooth.

## Fingerstyle
@group Picking

{{chords: C | guitar piano}}


- **Fingerstyle** uses the fingers of the picking hand: thumb (p) for the lowest strings, index (i), middle (m) and ring (a). The little finger usually rests on the body.
- Common patterns: the Travis picking pattern (thumb on the bass, index and middle on the treble), the arpeggio pattern (p i m a), and the chord-melody approach.
- Technique: pluck with the fleshy part of the finger, keep the nail short, and let each finger rest on the next string after it plays.

Exercise: play p i m a on the open strings E A D G B E in a steady pattern, then play the Travis pattern over a C major chord.

## Fretting hand
@group Fretting and legato

{{chords: C | guitar piano}}


- The thumb sits behind the neck, roughly opposite the middle or index finger. Keep it relaxed; a thumb that clamps the neck causes tension in the whole hand.
- Use the fingertips, close to the fret wire, with the fingers arched. Flat fingers mute adjacent strings.
- One finger per fret: index 1, middle 2, ring 3, little finger 4. Keep the fingers spread so they can reach their frets without moving the hand.
- Fret with enough pressure to make the note sound clean, and no more. Excess force causes sharp or flat notes and fatigue.

Exercise: play 1-2-3-4 across frets 1 to 4 on each string, one finger per fret. Keep all four fingers down while you play, and release them one by one.

## Hammer-ons and pull-offs
@group Fretting and legato

{{notes: A C | guitar piano}}


- A **hammer-on** sounds a higher note by fretting it firmly without picking. A **pull-off** sounds a lower note by lifting a finger off a fretted note with a slight downward pluck of the finger.
- Technique: the hammer-on comes from the fingertip hitting the string, close to the fret. The pull-off is a snap of the fingertip sideways and downward, not a lift upward.
- Power: a strong hammer-on needs real force; a weak one is inaudible. Practice both at a volume you can hear clearly.

Exercise: play the pattern 5 (pick), hammer 7, pull-off 5, on each string, slowly, then speed it up. The phrase should be evenly loud.

## Trills
@group Fretting and legato

{{notes: F F# | guitar piano}}


- A **trill** alternates rapidly between two adjacent notes using hammer-ons and pull-offs, with one pick at the start.
- Example: frets 5 and 6 on string 3, using fingers 1 and 2 (or 3 and 4). Pick the first note, then alternate hammer and pull for the length of the note.
- Speed comes from the fingertips, not the wrist. Keep the fingers close to the fret.

Exercise: trill frets 5 and 6 for four beats, then eight beats, then sixteen. Increase the length and the speed.

## Slides
@group Fretting and legato

- A **slide** moves the fretting finger along the string from one fret to another, with the note sounding continuously. Pick the first note and slide to the target fret.
- Types: legato slide (no pick on the second note), shift slide (pick on both notes), and a slide into a note from below.
- Practice the slide with a steady pressure, and keep the note in tune at the end.

Exercise: slide from fret 5 to fret 7 on the B string, picking only the first note. Then pick both notes and compare.

## Bends
@group Fretting and legato

{{notes: D E | guitar piano}}


- A **bend** raises the pitch of a fretted note by pushing the string sideways or up. A full bend is roughly a whole tone (two semitones); a half bend is about a semitone.
- Technique: use the strongest fingers (ring finger, or the middle and ring together). Push with the fingertip from the side of the string, not the top.
- Check the bend against a reference note: bend fret 7 on the G string toward the note played on fret 9 of the same string, and match the pitch by ear.
- Release: bring the string back down smoothly to the original note. Practise bends and releases with a tuner.
- **Pre-bend**: bend the string before picking, then pick and release to the target note.
- **Unison bend**: bend a note on one string to match the pitch of an open or fretted note on the next string. Common in rock and blues.

Exercise: bend the G string at fret 7 up a whole step, holding for two beats, then release. Repeat for a half step. Then do a unison bend: bend the G string at fret 5 (C) up a whole step until it matches the B string at fret 3 (D), and check it by ear.

## Vibrato
@group Fretting and legato

- **Vibrato** is the controlled wobble of pitch. Finger vibrato moves the fingertip along the string, wrist vibrato rotates the hand, arm vibrato moves the whole forearm.
- Vibrato width is the distance of the pitch change (narrow for jazz, wide for blues and rock). Speed is the number of waves per second.
- Practice: play the note, then rock the fingertip along the string at a steady rate. Start slow and steady, then speed up without losing the centre pitch.

Exercise: hold a note at fret 7 on the high E string, and produce a steady vibrato for eight beats at 60 BPM, then 120 BPM.

## Muting and palm muting
@group Muting and harmonics

{{notes: E | guitar piano}}


- **Palm muting** rests the side of the picking hand on the strings just before the bridge. The sound is short and percussive. Used in rock and metal riffs.
- Left-hand muting: a finger lightly touching the strings you are not playing stops unwanted noise.
- Muting is part of the sound. A good palm mute is consistent in pressure and placement.

Exercise: play a palm-muted eighth-note riff on string 6 (E, E, G, E, B, E) at 100 BPM. Keep the mute consistent: same place, same pressure.

## Pinch harmonics
@group Muting and harmonics

{{notes: G | guitar piano}}


- A **pinch harmonic** is produced by touching the string lightly with the side of the picking thumb just after the pick strikes it. The thumb damps the fundamental and lets the harmonic ring.
- Technique: the pick hits the string with its tip, and the side of the thumb touches the string at the same instant. The result is a bright, squealing tone.
- Practice on the open G string first. Without the left hand, just aim for the change in tone.
- The thumb contact should be light, not a forceful stop. Move the thumb slightly away from the pick until you find the harmonic.
- The sweet spot differs from guitar to guitar. Once you can do it on open strings, move to fretted notes.
- Pitch: the harmonic sounds above the fretted note. The exact pitch depends on where the thumb touches the string, so it can be an octave or more above the fretted note. Use a tuner to find the notes you can produce.

Exercise: produce a pinch harmonic on the open G string ten times in a row. Then do it on fret 5 and fret 7 of the same string.

## Natural harmonics
@group Muting and harmonics

{{notes: E B A | guitar piano}}


- A **natural harmonic** is produced by lightly touching the string directly over fret 12, 7 or 5, then picking. The note sounds an octave (fret 12), an octave and a fifth (fret 7), or two octaves (fret 5) above the open string.
- Use them for tuning: the harmonic at fret 12 should match the fretted note at fret 12. If they differ, the intonation is off.
- Harmonics sound clear and bell-like. Play them in lines by moving the finger between the harmonic points.

Exercise: play the natural harmonics at frets 5, 7 and 12 on each string. Name the notes you hear, then check them against the open string.

## Artificial harmonics
@group Muting and harmonics

{{notes: G | guitar piano}}


- An **artificial harmonic** is made by fretting a note with the fretting hand and touching a point 12 frets above with the picking hand's finger or the side of the index finger. The pick then sounds the harmonic.
- Technique: pick with the pick, touch lightly with the other finger, and move it along the string to find the harmonic. It sounds cleanly at the 12-fret point above the fretted note.
- Use them for melodic harmonics and for pitch shifts.

Exercise: fret the note on string 6 at fret 3, touch the string 12 frets above (fret 15), and pick. Name the harmonic. Repeat on other frets.

## Tapping
@group Fretting and legato

{{notes: A C E | guitar piano}}


- **Tapping** sounds notes by hammering a finger of the picking hand onto a fret, then pulling off or hammering the other hand's fingers.
- Two-hand tapping: the fretting hand plays the bass or chords, the picking hand taps the melody. Used in rock and fusion.
- Technique: tap with the tip of the middle or index finger of the picking hand, firm and close to the fret. Lift quickly to avoid buzzing. The tapping finger stays on the fretboard.
- Single-hand tapping: the picking hand taps and pulls off, the fretting hand holds a chord. Good for arpeggios.
- Practice: a three-note pattern (tap 12, pull to 10, pull to 8) on one string. Then add the second finger.

Exercise: play the arpeggio of A minor (A C E) using tapping on the 12th fret of strings 1 and 2 with pull-offs. Start at 60 BPM, increase slowly.

## Legato and sweep combinations
@group Fretting and legato

- Combining hammer-ons, pull-offs and slides with alternate picking gives a legato line. Use the picking hand only for the notes that need a fresh attack.
- Typical legato lick: pick 5, hammer 7, pull 5, slide 9. The sound flows.

Exercise: play a legato lick in A minor pentatonic, picking only every fourth note.

## Tremolo picking
@group Picking

- **Tremolo picking** repeats one note very fast with the pick. Technique: relax the wrist, and let the pick move in a small arc.
- Use it for rock and metal sustains and for flamenco.

Exercise: tremolo-pick the open G string for eight beats at 120 BPM, then 140 BPM.

## Whammy and tremolo bar
@group Fretting and legato

- A **tremolo bar** (whammy bar) changes the pitch of all the strings. Press and release smoothly. Overuse goes out of tune; keep the movement small.

Exercise: play a chord and move the bar gently up and down, returning to the original pitch.

## Chord-melody and comping
@group Chords and shapes

{{chords: C Am F G | guitar piano}}
{{chords: Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 | guitar piano}}


- **Comping** is accompanying: playing chords in the rhythm of the music. Use shell voicings, drop-2 chords or rootless chords so that the bass and the melody have room.
- **Chord-melody** plays the melody and the harmony at the same time, with the melody on top. Use the top string for the melody note and the lower strings for the chord.

## Progressions on the fretboard
@group Styles and progressions

The music theory file lists the standard progressions of each style (group **Progression standards**). This topic shows how a guitarist finds them quickly.

### Roots on strings 6 and 5

- With a root on string 6 at fret n:
  - **IV** is on string 5 at the same fret n.
  - **V** is on string 5 at fret n + 2.
  - **vi** (relative minor) is on string 6 at fret n − 3 (or n + 9).
- So I – IV – V is one E-shape barre and two A-shape barres a whole step apart. In G: G at fret 3 (string 6), C at fret 3 (string 5), D at fret 5 (string 5).
- This box is the same in every key. It is the guitarist's version of the **Nashville numbers**: think 1 – 4 – 5 – 6, not letter names.

{{chords: G C D Em | guitar}}

### Circle-of-fifths movement

- When roots fall by fifths (ii – V – I, the ragtime progression, "Autumn Leaves"), alternate shapes: root on string 6, then root on string 5, then string 6 again. The hand barely moves.
- Example in C: Dm7 (root string 5, fret 5) – G7 (root string 6, fret 3) – Cmaj7 (root string 5, fret 3).
- With drop-2 voicings on strings 4–1, every chord of a cycle can be reached within two frets.

{{chords: Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 | guitar}}

{{chords: E7 A7 D7 G7 C | guitar}}

### Open-string keys

- Many styles are built around open strings:
  - Rock, country, folk and bluegrass favour **E, A, D and G**, where the open strings ring as roots and drones.
  - Flamenco uses the **E position** (por arriba) and **A position** (por medio), and a capo to change pitch without changing shapes.
  - Celtic accompanists often retune to **DADGAD**, so open strings make drones in D.
- A **capo** turns any open-string progression into another key with the same shapes: C – Am – F – G with a capo at fret 2 sounds as D – Bm – G – A.

Example: the Mixolydian rock progression I – ♭VII – IV – I in D is D – C – G – D, all open chords.

Exercise: play I – IV – V – vi in G, C, D and A using only the string 6 / string 5 box. Say the numbers aloud, not the letters.

Exercise: play the "Autumn Leaves" cycle (Cm7 – F7 – B♭maj7 – E♭maj7 – Am7♭5 – D7♭9 – Gm) alternating string 5 and string 6 roots, without moving more than three frets.

Sources: the music theory file, **Progression standards** group; Wikipedia, [Nashville Number System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System); Moore, [The Flamenco Key](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~moore/solbul.pdf) (positions); [Slow Players, modes and keys](https://slowplayers.org/modes-keys/) (DADGAD).

## Blues guitar: inflections and positions
@group Styles and progressions

### String inflections

- A **string bend** raises a fretted note by pushing the string sideways. Blues players bend most often by a semitone or a whole tone, and sometimes by a tone and a half.
- A **bend and release** gives three pitches from one pluck: the note, the bent note, and the note again.
- Many blues bends are **between** the equal-tempered notes. One study counts over a hundred inflections in a single period of Lonnie Johnson's recordings, many of them not matching equal temperament.
- **The "blue third":** the third of the key, played somewhere between minor and major. Ethnomusicologist Gerhard Kubik describes this "neutral" third as roughly 320 to 360 cents above the root (a minor third is 300, a major third 400). On guitar this is a quarter-tone bend of the minor third.
- T-Bone Walker changed his wound G string for a plain one so that he could bend more: an early example of choosing strings for the style.

{{notes: A C | guitar}}

### Positions and motion

- The pentatonic boxes are tied to chord shapes. The study maps the scale onto the C, A and G shapes of the CAGED system, each within a four-fret span.
- Lonnie Johnson often linked two of these areas by **sliding with the third finger** along one string.
- The study's central idea: blues lines come from the **layout of the fretboard and the movements of the hand** as much as from scales. Players develop motives (short figures) for each of the I, IV and V chords and vary them.
- The **blue fifth** (♭5) is used sparingly but in an attention-drawing way, which may be why writers have given it so much weight.

{{scale: A minor pentatonic | guitar}}

{{scale: A blues | guitar}}

Example: over A7 in an A blues, bend C (string 3, fret 5) up a quarter tone toward C♯ and release. Over D7, aim for C as the seventh of D7 without bending.

Exercise: write one short motive for each chord of a 12-bar blues in A (A7, D7, E7). Play the whole form using only those three motives, varied each time.

Exercise: play C on string 3 (fret 5), then bend it slowly from C to C♯ and stop at three points on the way. Sing each pitch. Decide which one sounds most "blue" over an A7 chord.

Sources: Nelson, *Melodic Improvisation on a Twelve-Bar Blues Model* thesis (City, University of London), on Lonnie Johnson and T-Bone Walker, citing Kubik (1999); Open Music Theory v2, "Blues Harmony".

## Jazz guitar: comping the standard progressions
@group Styles and progressions

- **Shell voicings** on the low strings: root on string 6 or 5, plus the third and seventh. They outline the harmony and leave room for a pianist or soloist.
  - For a ii – V – I in C: Dm7 (D on string 5, F and C above), G7 (G on string 6, F and B), Cmaj7 (C on string 5, E and B).
- **Drop-2 voicings** on strings 4–1 or 3–1 for a lighter, higher sound. Keep common tones on the same string and move other voices by step.
- **Guide-tone line:** in a ii – V – I, the seventh of each chord falls a half step to the third of the next. Play only those two notes on strings 3 and 2 and listen to the line.
- Apply the same shapes to the jazz progressions in the theory file:
  - **Turnarounds:** C – A7 – Dm7 – G7, then the tritone version C – E♭7 – Dm7 – D♭7 (the bass moves by half step).
  - **Backdoor:** Fm7 – B♭7 – Cmaj7.
  - **Minor ii – V:** Dø – G7♭9 – Cm6.
  - **"So What":** quartal voicings (stacked fourths) on strings 4–1 over Dm7 and E♭m7.

{{chords: Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 | guitar}}

{{chords: C Eb7 Dm7 Db7 | guitar}}

{{chords: Dø G7b9 Cm6 | guitar}}

Example: comp a two-bar turnaround in C: Cmaj7 (two beats), A7 (two beats), Dm7 (two beats), G7 (two beats), with each chord short and on beats 2 and 4.

Exercise: play the "Autumn Leaves" cycle with shell voicings only, then with drop-2 voicings on strings 4–1. Record both and compare.

Sources: Open Music Theory v2, "Jazz Voicings" and "ii–V–I"; the music theory file, **Jazz standard progressions**.

## Brazilian guitar: samba, choro and the seven-string
@group Styles and progressions

### The samba levada

- A **levada** is the rhythmic accompaniment pattern played by the guitarist's right hand.
- The roles are split, like a percussion section:
  - The **thumb** plays the bass notes, in the role of the surdo (bass drum).
  - The **fingers** play short block chords on the higher strings, in the rhythm of the tamborim.
- The best-known tamborim pattern, called **teleco-teco** by musicians, groups 16 pulses as 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3. The name itself is the rhythm: "te-co" is a group of two, "te-le-co" a group of three.
- In partido alto, the same pattern appears in the pandeiro, rotated, so guitar and percussion cross and complete each other.
- Chord changes are often **anticipated**: they arrive a sixteenth before the bar. In a progression such as F – D7 – Gm – C7, the guitarist's chord pulls fall on the three-pulse groups.

{{chords: F D7 Gm C7 | guitar}}

### Choro and the seven-string guitar

- The **seven-string guitar** adds a low string below the sixth. Traditionally it is tuned to **C**; some players argue for **B**, which continues the fourths between the bass strings. Modern strings make C, B and even A possible.
- **Dino Sete Cordas** (from the 1950s–60s) defined the traditional style, with a steel seventh string played with a thumbpick for volume. Later players moved to nylon strings for more tone colour; Raphael Rabello extended the style with solo virtuosity.
- **Baixaria** is the bass counter-melody that answers the melody:
  - It works as **question and answer** with the soloist.
  - It leads the changes between sections and the returns to the A section.
  - Some baixarias are fixed by the composer ("baixaria de obrigação") and appear in the score.
  - **Slurs** (hammer-ons and pull-offs) are essential: the note is plucked once and the left hand links it to the next.
  - Modern players colour baixarias with substitutions, for example an arpeggio of G♭7 (the tritone substitute) before F instead of the diatonic C7.
- Choro harmony (diminished passing chords, secondary dominants, I – VI7 – ii – V) is covered in the theory file under **Brazilian standard progressions**.

{{chords: C C#o7 Dm7 G7 | guitar}}

### Bossa nova guitar

- The guitar moves in **four careful voices**, and many chord choices follow from smooth voice leading rather than from the melody.
- Leaving out the third of a chord makes it ambiguous, a sound bossa nova guitarists use on purpose.
- The thumb keeps a steady bass on beats 1 and 2 (in 2/4), while the fingers play syncopated chords.

Example: a samba levada on F – D7 – Gm – C7: thumb on the root of each chord on the beat, fingers on the top three strings following 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3, with each new chord arriving a sixteenth early.

Exercise: clap the 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 pattern while saying "te-co te-co te-le-co te-co te-co te-co te-le-co". Then play it with your fingers on one chord, with your thumb on beats 1 and 2.

Exercise: write a two-bar baixaria that leads from G7 to C, ending on the C in the bass. Use at least one slur.

Sources: Livramento, the guitar in samba (UDESC, Portuguese), for the levada, teleco-teco and partido alto; Borges, the seven-string guitar in choro (UnB, Portuguese), for tuning, strings and baixaria, citing Braga (2004) and Carrilho; Gava, The Harmonic Language of Bossa Nova (UNESP, Portuguese).

## Flamenco guitar: positions, compás and right hand
@group Styles and progressions

### Positions

- Flamenco guitarists think in **positions**, not keys. Each position has its own chord shapes and open-string colours; a capo moves it to suit the singer.
  - **Por arriba** (E position): Am – G – F – E. The home chord is E major, over a Phrygian scale.
  - **Por medio** (A position): Dm – C – B♭ – A.
- See **Flamenco harmony** in the theory file for the mode, the Andalusian cadence and the 12-beat compás.

{{chords: Am G F E | guitar}}

{{chords: Dm C Bb A | guitar}}

### Right-hand techniques

- **Rasgueado:** strumming with the fingers, often flicking them out one after another for a rolling burst. Used to accompany song and dance.
- **Picado:** single-note runs played with rest strokes, alternating index and middle fingers.
- **Tremolo:** the flamenco pattern is p – i – a – m – i (thumb, then four notes on the treble string). The classical tremolo is p – a – m – i, with three treble notes.
- **Alzapúa:** a thumb technique that uses down- and up-strokes, thought to come from plectrum playing. Considered one of the hardest techniques.
- **Golpe:** tapping the soundboard with a finger, usually on a tap-plate (golpeador) that protects the top.
- Many flamenco players support the guitar with the upper right arm at the elbow, which frees the hand for these techniques.

Example: in por arriba, rock between E and F with rasgueados on the accents of the compás (3, 6, 8, 10, 12), then play the Andalusian cadence once to close.

Exercise: play the flamenco tremolo (p – i – a – m – i) on string 1 over an open E bass, slowly, until all four treble notes are even. Then compare with the classical p – a – m – i.

Sources: [Richter Guitar, flamenco techniques and definitions](https://richterguitar.com/flamenco-guitar/flamenco-guitar-techniques-and-definitions/); Wikipedia, [Rasgueado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasgueado) and [Flamenco guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamenco_guitar); Moore, [The Flamenco Key](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~moore/solbul.pdf). The techniques are described here only in outline; learning them properly needs a teacher or a dedicated method.

## Rhythm guitar across styles
@group Styles and progressions

How the same few chords change character with the right-hand pattern. Each style's progressions are in the theory file's **Progression standards** group.

- **Reggae:** short chords on the off-beats (the skank), usually small triads on the top three or four strings, cut short by releasing the left-hand pressure. Bass and drums carry the beats. Progressions are simple: I – IV, I – IV – V – IV.
- **Rock:** power chords and open chords, often on modal progressions such as I – ♭VII – IV (D – C – G) or ♭VI – ♭VII – I (A♭ – B♭ – C).
- **Metal:** palm-muted power chords on a low pedal tone, with Aeolian (E5 – C5 – D5), Phrygian (E5 – F5) or tritone (E5 – B♭5) moves.
- **Country and bluegrass:** "boom-chick" (bass note on the beat, chord strum after), with alternating bass between root and fifth, on I – IV – V and II7.
- **Celtic:** open drones and power chords in DADGAD, with a minor home chord and a major chord a step below (Em – D) for Dorian tunes.
- **Salsa and Latin:** guitar parts follow the clave and the piano montuno; see **Afro-Cuban clave and salsa** in the theory file.

## Genre-specific technique
@group Genres and practice

- **Blues**: bends (half and full), vibrato, the minor pentatonic with the blue note (♭5), call and response, the shuffle rhythm (triplet feel).
- **Rock**: power chords, palm muting, pentatonic riffs, double-stop bends, hammer-ons and pull-offs for speed.
- **Metal**: gallop picking (down-up-down pattern on fast eighths), palm muting, pinch harmonics, tremolo, down-tuned strings.
- **Jazz**: ii–V–I voicings with extensions, the guide-tone (3rd and 7th) line, single-note lines in modes, the chord-melody approach.
- **Country**: hybrid picking, bends with the pedal steel feel, the Travis picking pattern, the open chords.
- **Flamenco**: rasgueado (fanned strumming with the fingers), tremolo, golpe (tapping the body), Phrygian scales, the Andalusian cadence.
- **Fingerstyle folk**: Travis picking, alternate bass patterns, open tunings.

For each style's chord progressions and a deeper look at blues, jazz, Brazilian and flamenco guitar, see the **Styles and progressions** group.

## Practice map
@group Genres and practice

- Name every note on the neck within the first position (frets 0 to 4). Then name the notes on frets 5 to 9, and then 9 to 12.
- Play a major scale in one position, then the next. Say each note aloud, and sing the interval to the next.
- Practise one triad on every string set, and name the chord tones and their roles.
- Use the metronome: start at 60 BPM, increase by 5 when the tempo is clean for a full minute.
- Record yourself at each tempo. Listen for uneven volume and buzzing.
