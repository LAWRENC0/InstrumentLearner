# Trumpet

Everything specific to the B♭ trumpet: transposition, valves and slides, the harmonic series, embouchure, air, tonguing, range, pedal tones, flexibility, intonation, mutes and practice. Read the music theory file alongside this one, especially the sections on intervals and the harmonic series.

How to use this file: read a topic, do its exercise with a tuner and a metronome, and write down what you hear. The **Styles and improvisation** group applies the theory file's progression library to the trumpet: chord symbols in written pitch, a blues practice order, jazz articulation and progression drills. Many trumpet skills are physical, so short daily sessions beat one long one.

A note on sources: this file mixes well-established principles with approaches that teachers disagree about. Where that happens, both views are given and marked **(disputed)**. Settle those questions with a method book such as Farkas's *The Art of Brass Playing* or a qualified teacher.

## Transposition: written and sounding pitch
@group Basics

{{notes: C E G | piano}}
{{notes: D F# A | piano}}


- A B♭ trumpet is a transposing instrument. The written note sounds a major second (whole step) lower. Written C sounds B♭; written D sounds C.
- Concert pitch is what the listener hears. Written pitch is what the player reads. The app shows concert pitch by default; the "Bb trumpet" option shows written pitch.
- A trumpet part is written a major second higher than the concert line. A concert C major part is written in D major for trumpet, so the key signature has two more sharps (or two fewer flats).
- Intervals are identical in written and concert pitch, because both notes move by the same amount. This is why the Interval Shift game does not change with the Bb option.

Example: a concert line C – E – G – C (C major triad) is written D – F♯ – A – D for the trumpet.

Exercise: write a concert C major scale, then write the trumpet part in D major. Check that the intervals between notes are unchanged.

## Valves and slides
@group Basics

{{notes: Bb A G | piano}}


- Three piston valves change the length of tubing. Valve 1 lowers the pitch by 2 semitones, valve 2 by 1, valve 3 by 3.
- Combinations add the lowerings: 1+2 lowers by 3, 2+3 by 4, 1+3 by 5, 1+2+3 by 6.
- Each valve loop has a slide ring: pulling a slide lengthens its tube. The 1st and 3rd slides are the ones used for intonation in most charts. The main tuning slide sets the overall pitch.
- A lower-pitched note needs more tube length. The valve loops and the slides are measured in the same unit (the effect of a slide is the same as adding tubing), which is why a slide correction changes every note played with that valve.

Exercise: play the open G, then press valve 1 and valve 2 separately, then together. Write down the pitch of each.

## The harmonic series and partials
@group Basics

{{notes: Bb Bb F Bb D F Ab Bb | piano}}


- A trumpet is a brass instrument with a tube of about 1.47 m. The bore is mostly cylindrical and flares at the bell. The pitch you produce depends on how you buzz your lips and on the tube length the valves select.
- Each note is a **partial** of the harmonic series: partial 1 is the fundamental (the pedal), partial 2 is an octave above, partial 3 a fifth above that, and so on. Partial n sounds at n times the fundamental frequency.
- With the same valves, changing the lip buzz moves to a different partial. Valves and slides move the whole series.
- The harmonics are not equal to equal temperament. Partial 7 is about 31 cents flat of the equal-tempered note, which is why the app's seventh partial is shown as out of tune.

Example: with no valves, the partials sound roughly B♭ (fundamental), B♭ (octave), F (fifth), B♭ (two octaves), D (major third above that), F, A♭ (flat), B♭. The notes of a B♭ major chord appear naturally.

Exercise: play open partials 2 to 6 (lip slurs, no valves) and tune each one with a tuner. Note which one is most out of tune.

## Embouchure: the lip setup
@group Embouchure and air

{{notes: Bb | piano}}


- The **embouchure** is the set of lip, jaw and facial muscles that controls the buzz. It is the single most important skill on the trumpet.
- Mouthpiece placement: the rim sits on the lips with the upper lip and lower lip sharing the pressure. Many players place more of the mouthpiece on the upper lip; the exact ratio varies from player to player.
- Lip aperture: the opening between the lips when they buzz. A smaller aperture gives higher pitch with more resistance; a larger one gives lower pitch and more air.
- The corners (the sides of the mouth) hold the embouchure firm. A firm but not tight corner gives the most stable tone.
- **Disputed:** whether the lips should be centred on the mouthpiece or placed with more upper lip. Sources disagree. Centred placement is common in many schools; upper-lip placement is also used by many professional players. Choose the setup that gives you a stable, centred tone and a clear attack.

Exercise: buzz on the mouthpiece alone, starting with a low, relaxed buzz. Find the most stable buzz at a medium volume, then hold it for 30 seconds. Repeat each day, and do not force the pitch.

## Air and breath support
@group Embouchure and air

- Breath support comes from the diaphragm and the lower abdomen, not the chest or shoulders. Breathe low: the stomach expands and the ribs widen.
- Air speed controls pitch as much as the lips do. Faster air moves the note up through the partials; slower air moves it down.
- Blow a steady stream of air, not a burst. The pressure should feel supported, not pushed.
- Corner breathing: taking a quick breath through the corners of the mouth (the sides) when the lips are on the mouthpiece is a technique used in some schools. It is disputed as a regular practice; try it only if your teacher recommends it.

Exercise: breathe in for four counts and out for eight, very slowly, with a hiss. Then play a long tone on one note, aiming for a steady airflow for 20 seconds.

## Tonguing and articulation
@group Embouchure and air

- Tonguing starts a note cleanly. The tongue touches just behind the upper front teeth (the alveolar ridge), then releases to start the buzz.
- Syllables: "too", "tah" or "dah" for a normal attack. "Doo" softens the attack; "tu" is clear and fast. Try each to hear the change.
- Single tonguing: one stroke per note. Double tonguing ("tu-ku") and triple tonguing ("tu-tu-ku") are used in fast passages. Practise them slowly, with the tongue returning to the same place each time.
- Legato means the tongue barely interrupts the airflow. Slurs are played with no tonguing between notes.
- **Disputed:** the tongue position for high and low notes. Some sources describe the tongue arched high (a closer "ee" shape) for higher notes and a lower "ah" shape for low notes, and say the tip strikes behind the top teeth for high register. Other teachers describe the tongue as staying at the same position, moving only the air and lips. Try both on your instrument and keep what gives the cleaner sound.

Exercise: play one note eight times, tonguing each time with "tu" at the same tempo. Then do the same with "du", then "ta". Listen for the clearest attack.

## Range: low, middle and high
@group Range and tone

{{notes: Bb F Bb | piano}}


- Practical range for most players runs from about F♯3 (written, sounding E3) to about C6 (written, sounding B♭5). The app's range covers concert B♭1 to C6.
- **Low register** (roughly the pedal up to the first partials): the lips are relaxed and vibrate more, the air moves slowly and steadily, and the mouthpiece pressure is light. Low notes need air more than pressure.
- **Middle register** (about the staff range): the most common playing area. The balance of air and lip tension is at its most efficient here.
- **High register** (above the staff): higher partials need more lip compression and faster air. Teachers describe lip compression as squeezing the lips together, not pushing the mouthpiece harder into the face. Excess mouthpiece pressure makes the lips tire quickly.
- **Disputed:** the pivot. Some players move the trumpet slightly as they go up (bell moving down), which changes the angle of the lips on the mouthpiece. Others say the pivot should be kept small or avoided. Test with a mirror and a tuner.

Exercise: play a single slur from middle C upwards in half steps as high as you can keep a clean tone, with a fast, supported air stream. Stop when the tone breaks. Each day, aim for one extra note; never force it.

## Pedal tones
@group Range and tone

{{notes: Bb A G | piano}}


- A **pedal tone** is a fundamental (partial 1) played with the valves. Pedals sit in the bottom of the register.
- **Disputed:** their value for range. One view holds that pedals help set a good lip position and help build a stable embouchure. A counter view notes that many pedal players let their lips roll out and that pedals alone do not build the muscles needed for the high register.
- Practical routine most teachers accept: play pedals softly, with a relaxed embouchure and steady air, for short sessions. Then return to the middle register with the same setup.
- Pedal tones are played with the valves shown in the app's fingering charts: the pedal is the lowest note of each combination.

Exercise: play the pedal B♭ (no valves, soft), then the pedal with valve 2 (A), then valves 1+2 (G). Use a tuner. Hold each for four beats.

## Lip slurs
@group Range and tone

{{notes: Bb F Bb D F | piano}}


- A **lip slur** changes partial without changing valves. The lips alone move the pitch up or down the harmonic series.
- Slur exercise: play open partials 2 to 6 in one slurred phrase, then back down. Keep the air steady and the tone even.
- Lip slurs train the embouchure's flexibility and help you hear the harmonic series.

Exercise: slur from the open B♭ up to the octave and back, then add valves to shift the whole pattern to other keys (press valves 1+2 for G, valve 2 for A, and so on).

## Valve combinations and fingering alternatives
@group Fingerings and effects

{{notes: D | piano}}


- Many notes have several valve combinations, each with a different tuning. The conventional fingering is the one most in tune without slide help.
- Combinations 1+3 and 1+2+3 are the most out of tune; they often need the 1st and 3rd slides. Learn your own corrections.
- Alternative fingerings: a note can be played with an open valve on a higher partial, or with valves on a lower partial. The two may differ in tone colour and tuning.
- The app's Scale Fingering Chart lists the alternatives for each note in a scale. Pentatonic notes can be outlined to find the common fingerings.

Exercise: choose one note (for example the D). Play it on every partial and valve combination that produces it, and rank them by tuning and ease.

## Half valves, trills and valve effects
@group Fingerings and effects

{{notes: Bb A | piano}}


- **Half valve** (or half-valve) technique: the valve is pressed partway. It can change tuning and tone colour, and is used for special effects (a "shake" or a bent note). Keep the valve controlled, not loose.
- **Valve trill**: alternate two valve combinations quickly, for example open and valve 1 (a whole step between them), or valves 2 and 1+2. Press with the fingers, not the wrist.
- Fall (the note drops in pitch as the air slows) and doit (the note rises at the end): used as expressive ends. Practise by slowing the air and lowering the lips, or by raising the air speed.

Exercise: trill open and valve 2 (a semitone apart) for eight beats, then valve 1 and open (a whole step) for eight beats. Keep the trill even and in tune.

## Flutter tongue, growl and effects
@group Fingerings and effects

- **Flutter tongue**: the tongue rolls or vibrates as the air passes, producing a rough, fluttering sound. Practice by saying "rrr" into the mouthpiece with a steady air stream.
- **Growl** (singing and buzzing): sing a note while playing it. The effect is a split sound, used in jazz and contemporary music.
- **Mutes**: straight mute (sharper, quieter, slightly sharp in pitch), cup mute (softer, warmer), harmon mute (nasal, classic jazz). A mute changes the tuning, so adjust the slides and listen again.

Exercise: play one note with a straight mute, then with a cup mute, then with a harmon. Tune each with the tuner and note the slide change needed.

## Dynamics and articulation control
@group Range and tone

- Louder playing needs faster air and a firm but relaxed embouchure, not more mouthpiece pressure.
- Softer playing needs slow, steady air and a smaller aperture. Avoid letting the lips collapse.
- Crescendo and diminuendo: change the air speed smoothly. Practice by holding a long note and varying the dynamic over eight beats.

Exercise: play a long note from pianissimo to fortissimo over eight beats, then back to pianissimo. Keep the pitch stable throughout.

## Intonation and tuning
@group Intonation and practice

{{notes: Bb C D Eb F G A | piano}}


- Intonation is measured in cents from equal temperament. A note 20 cents sharp is audible; 50 cents is a quarter of a semitone and clearly wrong.
- Most intonation problems come from valve combinations, not from the lips. Pull a slide to make a note flatter, push it in to make it sharper.
- Use a tuner in practice. Play a long tone, adjust the slide only for the valves that need it, then check the whole range.
- Good intonation also depends on air speed: a note played too softly often goes flat.

Exercise: play a B♭ major scale with a tuner, noting each note's cents deviation. Correct only the notes outside 10 cents, then play it again.

## Practice routine: warm-up, long tones, flexibility
@group Intonation and practice

- Warm-up (5 minutes): breathe, buzz on the mouthpiece, play long tones on the middle register, then slur from low to high.
- Long tones (10 minutes): hold each note of a scale for eight beats, steady and in tune. Vary the dynamic.
- Flexibility (10 minutes): lip slurs between partials, then lip slurs with valves.
- Technique (15 minutes): scales in all keys, articulation patterns, one register study.
- Repertoire (rest of session): the music you need for your playing.
- Rest: take two minutes between sections. Stop before the lips tire; fatigue causes bad habits.

Exercise: write your own 40-minute routine using the sections above. Keep it for two weeks, then review what improved.

## Common problems and fixes
@group Intonation and practice

- Lips tire quickly: check mouthpiece pressure (too much), check air (too little), and rest more often.
- Notes are flat in the high register: increase air speed; do not push the mouthpiece in.
- Notes crack when you move between registers: practise the slur across the break slowly, with a steady air stream.
- Sharp in the low register: check the air, which may be too fast; relax the lips.
- Buzzing on the mouthpiece is unstable: check the embouchure setup in a mirror; relax the corners.

Exercise: record yourself on a scale, then name one problem in the recording and work on it for five minutes each day.

## Ear training for trumpet

- Sing before you play: sing each note of a scale before playing it, then check.

## Reading chord symbols on B♭ trumpet
@group Styles and improvisation

- Chord symbols on a trumpet part are transposed like the notes: **up a whole step** from concert pitch. A concert F7 is written G7.
- Rhythm-section players read concert pitch, so check which version a chart shows before you improvise.
- Common jazz keys and what the trumpet reads:
  - Concert F → written G.
  - Concert B♭ → written C.
  - Concert E♭ → written F.
  - Concert C → written D.
- The Roman numerals do not change. A concert ii – V – I in B♭ (Cm7 – F7 – B♭maj7) is written Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7, still ii – V – I.

### The blues in written pitch

- **Basic blues, concert F, written G:** G7 (4 bars) | C7 (2) | G7 (2) | D7 | C7 | G7 | D7.
- **Jazz blues, written G:** G7 | C7 | G7 | G7 | C7 | C7 | G7 | G7 | Am7 | D7 | G7 E7 | Am7 D7. Some players use Bm7 (iii) instead of G7 in bar 11.
- **Bird blues** (Charlie Parker's "Blues for Alice"), written G: it opens Gmaj7 | F♯ø B7 | Em7 A7 | Dm7 G7 | C7 …, a chain of ii – Vs that still reaches IV (C7) in bar 5 and ends like the jazz blues.

{{chords: G7 C7 D7 | piano}}

### Other standard progressions, written

- **Rhythm changes,** concert B♭, written C: C – A7 – Dm7 – G7 (A sections), E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 (bridge).
- **Minor ii – V – i,** concert G minor, written A minor: Bø – E7♭9 – Am.
- **Andalusian cadence,** concert A minor, written B minor: Bm – A – G – F♯.

Exercise: write out a concert F jazz blues in written pitch, bar by bar. Then do the same for concert B♭ (written C).

Sources: Jazz Theory (MTC130), "The Blues" and "The Blues: Improv Strategies" (B♭ instrument editions); the music theory file, **The blues** and **Jazz standard progressions**.

## Improvising on the blues: a trumpet practice order
@group Styles and improvisation

This order comes from the course handouts for B♭ instruments. Concert F blues = **written G blues**.

1. **Minor pentatonic** of the written key over the whole form: G B♭ C D F.
2. **Triads** of each chord (G, C, D), in different inversions. Improvise with triad notes only.
3. **Four-note arpeggios** (1 3 5 7): up, down, and up one chord, down the next.
4. **Guide tones:** the third and seventh of each chord, connected chord to chord.
   - G7: B and F. C7: E and B♭. D7: F♯ and C. Am7: C and G.
   - From G7 to C7, the seventh F falls a half step to the third E; the third B falls a half step to B♭, the seventh of C7.
5. **Chord scales:** Mixolydian on the dominant chords, Dorian on the minor chords.
6. **Connect the guide tones** with scale notes in between, by half step where possible. Write out your own solos this way.

### Building blocks to practise every day

From *A Guide to Exploring Jazz Improvisation*, which is written for any instrument:
- **Scales:** all twelve major scales, plus Dorian and Mixolydian, at quarter note = 92 or slower. Cycle through keys in different orders (round the circle, by half step, by whole step).
- **Triads:** 1 3 5 up through the keys in half steps, then 5 3 1, then alternating up and down, then spaced in whole steps and in fourths. Then minor, diminished and augmented, then seventh chords.
- **Intervals:** one interval a week, from minor thirds up to major sevenths, moved through the keys.
- **Rhythm:** off-beats; groups of four or five eighth notes followed by an eighth rest (so the pattern moves across the beat); triplets. Apply each rhythm to scales and triads, then to a tune.

Example: over written G7 – C7, play the line D – E – F (G7) then E (C7): the last note of G7 is its seventh, falling by half step to the third of C7.

Exercise: play a whole written G blues using only guide tones as long notes. Then fill the gaps with scale notes, keeping the guide tones on beat 1 of each bar.

Sources: Jazz Theory (MTC130), "The Blues: Improv Strategies" (B♭ trumpet edition); Kelly, Rasmussen and Roberts, *A Guide to Exploring Jazz Improvisation, Book 1* (CC BY 4.0).

## Jazz articulation and swing on trumpet
@group Styles and improvisation

- Jazz articulation is lighter and more legato than classical. Teachers often replace the classical "ta" with a softer **"da"** or **"doo"**.
- A common set of syllables (conventions differ between teachers):
  - **"Doo":** long notes on the beat.
  - **"Dah":** accented long notes, on or off the beat.
  - **"Dn"** (or "nn"): **ghost notes**, swallowed so they are barely heard.
- Swing eighths are played long–short (roughly 2:1, see **Rhythm and meter** in the theory file). Many teachers say the special jazz articulation rules apply **only to eighth notes**.
- A widely taught habit: tongue the off-beat eighths and slur into the next on-beat, so the off-beats are lightly accented. This gives the line its swing.
- Lead trumpeter Mike Lovatt teaches using a soft "d" tongue to accent notes inside a legato line, rather than slurring everything.
- Dynamics move constantly inside a phrase; jazz phrasing is shaped by small changes of volume.
- Expressive effects (falls, doits, shakes, growl, mutes) are covered in **Half valves, trills and valve effects** and **Flutter tongue, growl and effects**.

Example: a swung written-G scale: G (doo) A (dah, tongued off-beat) slurred to B, C (tongued off-beat) slurred to D, and so on, with every off-beat slightly accented.

Exercise: play a written G major scale in swung eighths three ways: all tongued, all slurred, then tonguing the off-beats and slurring to the on-beats. Record all three and listen for which one swings.

Sources: Florida Bandmasters Association, [How to "Doo-Dit": classical vs jazz articulation](https://fba.flmusiced.org/media/2648/how-to-doo-dit-classical-vs-jazz-articulation.pdf); [Mike Lovatt, articulation in swing style (MusicGurus)](https://www.musicgurus.com/courses/explore-swing-style-on-the-trumpet-mike-lovatt/lessons/articulation-slinky-swing-style-trumpet-mike-lovatt); [Hickey's jazz method sample](https://www.hickeys.com/samples/094/sku94937.pdf); Open Music Theory v2, "Swing Rhythms". These are teaching materials rather than research, and teachers disagree on the exact syllables.

## Playing the progression library on trumpet
@group Styles and improvisation

A trumpet plays one note at a time, so the way to learn a progression is to **arpeggiate it** and to find a **guide-tone line** through it. All examples are in written pitch.

- **Arpeggio drill:** play each chord of the progression as 1 3 5 7, one chord per bar, then 7 5 3 1, then alternate. Keep the time steady.
- **Guide-tone drill:** play only the third or seventh of each chord, choosing whichever is closest to the last note.
- Progressions to work through (from the theory file's **Progression standards**):
  - **ii – V – I:** Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7. Guide tones F – F – E, C – B – B.
  - **Minor ii – V – i:** Bø – E7♭9 – Am. Over E7♭9 use E Phrygian dominant (the fifth mode of A harmonic minor).
  - **Turnaround:** C – A7 – Dm7 – G7. The C♯ in A7 leads up to D.
  - **Ragtime progression:** E7 – A7 – D7 – G7 – C. Each third falls a half step to the next seventh.
  - **Andalusian cadence:** Am – G – F – E (in E Phrygian with G♯ on the last chord).
  - **Bossa nova "Ipanema" move:** Cmaj7 – D7 – Dm7 – D♭7. Over D7 play D Lydian dominant (with G♯); over D♭7, aim for C♭ (B) and F, the guide tones shared with G7.
- Brass and the harmonic series: arpeggios that sit on one valve combination (for example C – E – G on open valves, written) are the natural starting point; the others need clean valve changes.

{{chords: Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 | piano}}

{{chords: E7 A7 D7 G7 C | piano}}

Example: the guide-tone line through written C – A7 – Dm7 – G7 – C: E (third of C) – C♯ (third of A7) – C (seventh of Dm7) – B (third of G7) – C.

Exercise: choose three progressions from the list. Play each one as arpeggios for four choruses, then as a guide-tone line, then improvise using only chord tones on the beat.

Sources: the music theory file, **Jazz standard progressions**, **Brazilian standard progressions** and **Flamenco harmony**; Jazz Theory (MTC130) handouts; Kelly, Rasmussen and Roberts, *A Guide to Exploring Jazz Improvisation, Book 1*.

## Practice map
@group Intonation and practice
